A normal photo is a flat grid of pixels. A Gaussian splat is different: it stores a scene as a cloud of soft, translucent, coloured blobs floating in 3D space. Because the blobs are in 3D, you can draw the scene from any angle, not just where the photos were taken. This page learns those blobs from photos, live, using your graphics card (via WebGPUi).




Start with fuzz20,000 random blobs scattered in a ball. Nothing looks like anything yet.


Draw and comparePick one training photo, draw the blobs from that camera angle, and measure how different the picture is from the real photo.


Nudge every blobMath (calculus) tells each blob how to move, resize, rotate, change colour and opacity so the picture gets closer. The step repeats thousands of times.


Add and removeWhere detail is missing, blobs split or copy themselves. Useless blobs are deleted. The scene sharpens.


Clean up and saveSurfaces are flattened (PCAsplat), weak blobs are pruned, and you can download the result.






 
Every technique on this page, what it means in everyday words, where it comes from, and how it is used here. “Adapted” means the idea comes from the paper and was rebuilt for this trainer. Everything runs on your graphics card and was written from scratch for this page.

  


3D Gaussian Splatting
Kerbl et al., 2023 · arXiv 2308.04079core

Store the scene as blobs and draw them by “splatting” each blob onto the screen as a soft ellipse, nearest first, like stacking tinted glass.
Here: a full rewrite in WebGPU shaders, including the maths that tells each blob how to improve (the “backward pass”), verified against numerical checks.


Tile-based sorted rasteriser
Kerbl et al., 2023 · arXiv 2308.04079speed

The picture is cut into 16×16-pixel tiles. Each tile only considers the blobs that touch it, sorted front to back, so thousands of tiles are drawn in parallel.
Here: tile lists are built and depth-sorted on the GPU every step.


Subgroup (warp-level) gradient sums
Adapted from VkSplat · arXiv 2605.00219speed

Many GPU threads must add their “how should this blob change” numbers together. Doing the adding inside tight groups of threads, instead of through slow shared memory, is much faster.
Here: used automatically when your GPU supports it (the backward pass went from about 8 ms to 3 ms per step); a slower fallback runs otherwise.


PCAsplat: local PCA regulariser
Matias et al., 2026 · arXiv 2610.11011geometry

For every blob, look at its 16 nearest neighbours and ask “do these form a thin sheet?” (PCA, principal component analysis, finds the sheet's direction). Blobs are then nudged to lie flat on it and to face the same way as their neighbours.
Here: eigenvalues from a 3×3 solver on the GPU, the paper's λ₁ + γ(λ₃−λ₂) loss, plus a normal-alignment term and a surface-flatness term, ramped up from weight 1 to 10 in the later 70% of training. Measured here: surfaces about 3× thinner and 96% aligned, for roughly a 0.7 dB sharpness cost on clean synthetic data.


View-dependent colour (spherical harmonics)
Kerbl et al., 2023 · arXiv 2308.04079quality

Real surfaces change colour as you walk around them: a shiny highlight slides across plastic. “Spherical harmonics” are a compact recipe for that change, so each blob can look slightly different from different angles.
Here: degree-1 harmonics (9 extra numbers per blob) with their own gradient and optimiser kernel, switched on after 20% of training. Measured in a quick test: about +0.9 dB with no speed cost. Saved in the .ply file in the standard format.


SSIM structural loss
Wang et al., 2004; used by 3DGS · arXiv 2308.04079quality

Plain pixel error (“how different is each pixel?”) can accept blur. SSIM instead checks local contrast and structure in small windows, so edges and texture are rewarded.
Here: an 11×11 Gaussian-window SSIM with a hand-derived gradient computed in two extra GPU passes, weighted 0.03 against the pixel loss, with a matching densification threshold. Its gradient was checked against finite differences. Measured on the Lego scene: about +0.6 dB with slightly fewer blobs.


Squared-error loss (instead of absolute)
Idea from Polarized Opacity Prior · arXiv 2608.22344quality

Measuring mistakes by their square punishes big errors harder and lets small blobs get cheaper, giving a more compact scene at the same sharpness.
Here: adopted as the default pixel loss (about +1.3 dB over absolute error in my early tests). The paper's opacity-polarising term is also implemented, and is off by default because it did not help in this scene.


Random seeding and splitting (escape “blur traps”)
Adapted from Blur Trap · arXiv 2607.17965quality

Blobs that are too big or too far away can get stuck blurry because the usual “clone where the error is” rule never triggers. Sprinkling a few fresh random blobs and splitting a few big ones gives the optimiser new chances.
Here: 20 random new blobs and 20 forced splits at every densification round.


Adaptive density control
Kerbl et al., 2023 · arXiv 2308.04079core

Blobs that keep getting pushed hard are in under-detailed areas: small ones are cloned, big ones are split in two. Nearly invisible or oversized blobs are deleted.
Here: runs on the CPU every 100 steps, keeps each surviving blob's optimiser memory, and respects your blob limit.


Coarse-to-fine training
Inspired by Laplacian Frequency Hierarchies · arXiv 2609.03334speed

Learn the big shapes from small, cheap pictures first, then add fine detail at full size. Early steps cost about a quarter as much.
Here: the first 35% of steps use half-resolution photos. (This is a simple two-level version of the idea, not the paper's Laplacian pyramid.)


Importance pruning
Adapted from LinearMask-GS · arXiv 2609.10095compact

Near the end, each blob is scored by the most it ever contributed to any training view. The weakest are deleted and the rest are fine-tuned, giving a smaller, faster file.
Here: tracked on the GPU every step with a running maximum over views, then the weakest 40% are cut at 88% of training. In tests this removes about 40% of the blobs for roughly 0.1 dB. (The paper learns a soft mask; this is the simpler “score then cut” variant.)


Error-guided sparse sampling (tested, switched off)
Adapted from TurboGS · arXiv 2606.15924tested, off

Only train on the patches of the picture that are currently most wrong, instead of the whole picture each step.
Implemented as a tile-level version (error map + “age” so no patch is forgotten, 16×16-pixel tiles). On an early Lego test scene it ran only 16% faster at half the tiles but lost 1 dB, so it is off by default. The paper's full pixel-level version with its own optimiser may do better; this simplified one did not.


Adam optimiser with learning-rate decay
Kingma & Ba, 2014 · arXiv 1412.6980core

The “learning rate” is how big each nudge is. Adam adapts it for every number separately, and the rate shrinks over time so the scene settles instead of jittering.
Here: fused into one GPU kernel; position steps decay 100× over the run.


Anti-aliasing low-pass filter
From 3DGS (EWA splatting) · arXiv 2308.04079quality

Every blob is drawn at least about one pixel wide so tiny blobs do not shimmer or alias.


Automatic camera calibration for your photos
Original to this pagephotos

Without a transforms.json, the page assumes your photos circle the object, tries a grid of camera heights and lens widths with a quick mini-training run for each, and keeps the combination that fits the photos best. During training it also wiggles each camera slightly and keeps changes that improve the match.
This is a lightweight stand-in for full “structure-from-motion”: it works for steady walk-arounds, not for random handheld photos.


Structure from motion: finding where each photo was taken
Classic computer vision: Harris corners (1988), BRIEF/ORB features (2010–11), Hartley & Zisserman’s multi-view geometry, bundle adjustment (Triggs et al., 2000)photos

To learn a 3D scene the trainer must know exactly where the camera was for every photo. Instead of asking you, the page finds corners that show up in several photos, matches them, and solves the geometry so that the camera positions, your lens's field of view and a sparse cloud of 3D points all agree. A final “bundle adjustment” nudges all of them together to shrink the leftover error.
Here: written from scratch in plain JavaScript: oriented BRIEF features, Hamming matching with ratio and mutual checks, 8-point essential-matrix RANSAC, cheirality-checked pose recovery, DLT PnP RANSAC for adding photos one by one, and Levenberg–Marquardt bundle adjustment with a shared free focal length. Measured on real photos with known answers: LLFF “fern” 1.5% camera-position error; on a real handheld fox video (50 frames) every frame is placed and the focal length lands within 0.6° of the truth. Using 3,000 features per photo and matching each photo against 20 partners placed all 50 frames, where 1,500 features and 10 partners placed only 31, at roughly 2.5 times the solve time. Joint camera refinement also keeps running during training.


Sharp-frame picking from video
Original to this page (variance-of-Laplacian blur measure)photos

Handheld video is full of motion-blurred frames, and one blurry frame smears detail into the scene. The page cuts the video into time slices, looks at four candidate frames in each, and keeps the one with the most fine detail.
Here: 48 slices, sharpness = variance of the Laplacian, kept frames are re-encoded as JPEG and sent through the same camera solver as photos.


One object or a whole scene
Original to this page; masked-training idea from Object-Centric 2DGS · arXiv 2501.08174photos

Scene mode keeps everything, for example a room interior, and starts the viewer at your first camera. Object mode finds the thing all the photos are aimed at, removes the floor, wall and clutter around it, and retrains on the cut-out so the detail goes into the object.
Here: the object is the set of 3D points near every camera’s line of sight; table, floor and wall planes are found with RANSAC; blobs behind a plane, flat against it, or outside the object’s projected outline in most photos are removed (a multi-view vote); then the photos are masked to white and training continues. The paper uses a mask loss from hand-made masks; here the masks are made automatically from the camera solution.


Maximum-quality mode
Measured on this trainer; densification ideas surveyed in arXiv 2508.12313quality

Pick Maximum (800 px) before adding your photos for the sharpest result: photos are trained at 800 pixels wide, up to 250,000 blobs may be grown, and blobs are allowed to split earlier. It takes about 4 times longer than Detailed.
Here: on a Lego test scene, 800 px with a lower splitting threshold reached 34.5 dB on unseen photos (180k blobs) versus 32.7 dB (46k blobs) for the default. The vintage-camera demo was trained this way: 30.8 dB on unseen photos, 156k blobs. Detail is always bounded by the sharpness of the photos you give it.


Per-photo exposure and white balance
Adapted from Robust Gaussian Splatting · arXiv 2404.04211qualityyour photos

Phone photos taken a few minutes apart often differ in brightness and colour, because the camera re-meters each shot. If the scene is forced to explain that, it comes out blotchy or full of ghosts. The trainer works out, for each photo, a simple brightness and colour correction and trains against the corrected photo.
Here: an alternating form of the paper's per-image affine colour: three times during training (at 25, 50 and 75%) the current render of each photo is compared with the photo, a robust per-channel gain and bias is fitted (outliers trimmed, gain limited to 0.7–1.4), blended with the previous estimate, and the training target is rewritten as (photo − bias) / gain. Only for your own photos; the demo scenes have fixed lighting. Its benefit on real photos has not been measured yet.


Sharper photos count for more
Simplified from ClearGS · arXiv 2609.31509qualityyour photos

In a handheld video some frames are blurry. Throwing them away leaves gaps in coverage; treating them like the sharp ones smears the result. Instead, blurry frames are shown to the trainer less often, but never dropped.
Here: each photo gets a sharpness score (variance of the Laplacian); the sampling weight is 0.3 + 0.7 · min(1, √(score / median)). The paper grades frames with a vision-language model and also restores blurry frames with a neural network; neither is done here. Its benefit has not been measured yet.

Compact demo files (visually lossless)
Ideas from KISS-GS · arXiv 2608.26948, Self-Organizing Gaussians · arXiv 2312.13299, Morton sorting · arXiv 2609.29041size

The built-in demo scenes would be about 9 MB each if stored plainly. Sorting the blobs so neighbours in 3D sit next to each other in the file, rounding each number to just the precision the eye can use, and then compressing the result makes them 2.3 times smaller with no visible change.
Here: blobs are ordered along a Morton (Z-order) curve; positions, sizes, rotations, colours, opacities and SH are quantised per channel with extreme outliers clipped; each channel is stored as separate byte planes, delta-coded and deflated, and decoded in your browser with the built-in DecompressionStream. Measured on a 175k-blob test scene: 9.1 MB to 4.0 MB, held-out quality 34.55 dB to 34.48 dB. The vintage-camera demo (156k blobs) is 3.6 MB. The papers go much further (image codecs, codebooks for colour, pruning-aware fine-tuning); those were not implemented.


Stop-when-done and GPU duty cycle
Original to this pageefficiency

Optional early stopping, a duty-cycle limiter (Gentle uses 30% of the GPU), a small default blob count on phones, and no per-frame readback keep the tab light.






 


Gaussian / blob / splat
A “Gaussian” is a soft, bell-shaped blob: opaque in the middle, fading smoothly at the edges. In 3D it can be stretched and rotated into an ellipsoid. “Splat” is the verb for drawing one on screen.


Training / iteration / step
Training is repeated trial and correction. One iteration (step) means: draw one photo's view, compare with the real photo, adjust the blobs a little.


PSNR (dB)
A score of how close two pictures are, in decibels. Higher is better; 30 dB looks good, each +1 dB means roughly 20% less error.


Held-out / test photos
Photos kept hidden during training and used only to grade the result, so the score cannot be inflated by memorisation.


Loss
A single number for “how wrong is the picture right now”. Training tries to push it down.


Gradient / backward pass
The gradient says, for every blob and every property, which direction to change it to reduce the loss. The “backward pass” is the calculation of those directions.


Densification and pruning
Densification adds blobs where detail is missing (by cloning or splitting). Pruning deletes blobs that are nearly invisible, giant, or unimportant.


Opacity
How see-through a blob is: 0 is invisible, 1 is solid.


Scale, rotation, covariance
The size and orientation of a blob. Together they define its ellipsoid shape (the maths calls this the covariance).


SH / view-dependent colour
Spherical harmonics let a blob change colour with viewing angle (a sheen or highlight that moves as you move). This page uses “degree 1”, a gentle tint that varies with direction, to stay fast and light.


PCA, surface normal, thickness
PCA finds the main directions of a small cloud of points. For a flat sheet the shortest direction is the surface normal (which way it faces) and its spread is the thickness.


Regulariser
An extra rule added to training to prefer sensible solutions, here “surfaces should be thin sheets”.


Floaters
Stray blobs that hang in mid-air and show up as ghostly specks from some angles. Cleaner geometry and pruning reduce them.


Camera pose / transforms.json
Where and how the camera stood for each photo. A transforms.json file lists them. Training needs poses to know where each photo was taken from.


Orbit prior
A guess that your photos were taken walking in a circle around the object at a steady distance, used when you do not supply poses.


WebGPU
A browser feature that lets web pages use your graphics card for computation. Without it, this page cannot run.


Subgroup
A small team of GPU threads (often 32) that can share numbers very quickly. Used to add up gradients fast.


.ply and .splat
Two file formats for splats. .ply is the common standard (about 17 numbers per blob); .splat is a compact 32-byte-per-blob web format.


Structure from motion (SfM)
Working out where the camera stood for every photo, and the rough 3D shape of the scene, purely from the overlap between the photos.


Feature / keypoint / match
A feature is a distinctive spot, such as a corner, that can be recognised again in another photo. Matching pairs the same spot across photos; those pairs are the clues for the geometry.


RANSAC
A way of ignoring bad data: guess a solution from a few random clues, count how many other clues agree, and keep the guess most of them support. Wrong matches simply fail to agree.


Bundle adjustment
A final clean-up that moves every camera and every 3D point a tiny bit at once so that, when the points are projected back into the photos, they land as close as possible to where they were seen.


Focal length / field of view
How zoomed-in the lens is. A wide-angle lens has a large field of view. The camera solver measures it from your photos rather than asking you.


Reprojection error (px)
How far, in pixels, the solved 3D points land from where they were actually seen in the photos. Under about 1 px means the cameras fit the photos well.


Subject mode / scene mode
Subject mode cuts one object out of its surroundings. Scene mode keeps everything, such as a room.


Mask
A black-and-white picture saying which pixels belong to the object. Pixels outside it are painted white so the trainer ignores them.


Motion blur
Smearing caused by the camera moving while the shutter is open. Blurry photos teach the scene to be blurry, so they are avoided.


Held-out “Gaussian budget”
The cap on how many blobs training may create. A smaller cap means a smaller, faster scene with less fine detail.






 
Honest limits
    

  • View-dependent colour is only degree 1 (a gentle angle-dependent tint). Sharp mirror-like reflections need higher degrees, which I did not add to keep the scene small. .splat files carry no angle-dependent colour; use .ply to keep it.
    • 

  • The camera solver is a compact, from-scratch one, not COLMAP. It works best with smooth, overlapping coverage (about 5° or less between neighbouring photos) of a textured, non-flat scene. On the real fox video test, all 50 frames are placed with the default settings (an earlier, lighter setting placed only 31). Shots that jump a long way, mostly flat scenes (a wall, a table top) or rotating on the spot can fail; the page then falls back to assuming an orbit, which is much less reliable, or you can supply transforms.json from a tool like COLMAP or nerfstudio.
    • 

  • Object cut-out is automatic and imperfect. It works best when the object stands in front of its surroundings. On the hardest test, the fox pressed flat against a patterned wall, the head came out sharp but some wallpaper ghosts and a few holes remained (19.7 dB on unseen photos).
    • 

  • Detail cannot exceed the photos. Zooming far closer than the photos were taken shows the limits of the source, no matter the settings; Maximum mode simply stops the trainer from adding blur on top. Moving things in the scene (people, pets) are not removed in scene mode.
    • 

  • PCAsplat costs a little sharpness on clean synthetic data (about 0.7 dB here) in exchange for cleaner geometry; it should matter more on noisy real photos, which was not benchmarked.
    • 

  • Not included: learned (rather than rule-based) pruning masks and truncated-gradient revival of dead blobs (TruncGradGS). Sparse pixel sampling (TurboGS) was implemented and tested but turned off because it did not help here.
    • 

  • Needs a WebGPU browser and a real GPU; speed depends heavily on your hardware. Camera solving runs on the main thread and can take a few minutes for 50 photos. Blob counts above about 250,000 are not supported.
    •
